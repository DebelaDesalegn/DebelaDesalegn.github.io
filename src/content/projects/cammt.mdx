## 1. Research motivation

Culturally specific content is hard to translate because language alone may not carry enough context to capture region-specific meaning. Images can supply that context, but it was unclear whether vision–language models actually use it.

## 2. Problem statement

Measure whether adding an image improves machine translation of culturally grounded captions, and in which situations.

## 3. Related work

Multimodal machine translation grounds sentences in images, and benchmarks such as CoMMuTe evaluate visual disambiguation, but they do not address broader cultural nuance across many regions.

## 4. Research question

Can images act as cultural context in translation, and does visual grounding help preserve culturally specific items, disambiguate meaning and mark gender correctly?

## 5. Methodology

Question–answer pairs from CVQA were turned into declarative captions in English and each regional language. Native-speaker co-authors corrected them, checked that cultural items were kept, and labelled each caption as not culturally relevant, culturally relevant, or containing a cultural item. For captions with cultural items, annotators also wrote versions that conserve or substitute the item and chose their preferred one.

## 6. Experimental setup

Five vision–language models (Gemma 3 27B and 12B, two Qwen2.5-VL sizes including 32B, and Gemini 2.0 Flash) translated with and without the image, from each regional language to English and from English to each regional language. NLLB-3.3B served as a strong text-only baseline, and replacing the image with an unrelated one served as a control.

## 7. Dataset

5,817 image–caption triples across 19 languages and 23 region–language pairs, including Amharic and Oromo (Ethiopia), Swahili (Kenya) and Igbo (Nigeria), with a 1,550-sample subset for cultural-item analysis. Released on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/villacu/cammt).

## 8. Evaluation metrics

chrF++ (main metric), BLEU and BERTScore, plus human pairwise preference between text-only and image-augmented translations, with the reason for each choice.

## 9. Results

- Native speakers preferred the image-augmented translation in **85 of 126** comparisons (67.5%).
- chrF++ gains were small but consistent, clearest when translating into English (for example +2.5 for Qwen2.5-VL 32B).
- An unrelated image lowered scores, so the gains come from the image content.
- Preferences were driven by preserving cultural items, correct gender marking and disambiguation.
- The best vision–language models matched or beat NLLB-3.3B, especially into English.

## 10. Limitations

Dataset size is bounded by the CVQA source, each region had a single annotator, specialised multimodal MT systems were not evaluated, and human evaluation used pairwise preference only.

## 11. Future research directions

More samples and annotators per region, models that decide when visual context should shape a translation and ignore unrelated images, better evaluation of visual context, and script-aware handling of cultural items.
