## 1. Research motivation

Most Ethiopians are excluded from voice technology because existing speech recognition systems give little or no support to Ethiopian languages. As speech technology advances, this gap risks widening existing inequalities.

## 2. Problem statement

Build compact, open speech recognition models that also identify the language spoken, for five Ethiopian languages from the Semitic, Cushitic and Omotic branches of the Afroasiatic family.

## 3. Related work

Large multilingual systems such as Whisper, SeamlessM4T, MMS and OmniASR cover some of these languages, but with high error rates or very large model sizes.

## 4. Research question

Can a single multilingual CTC model jointly transcribe and identify these languages, and where do its errors come from?

## 5. Methodology

Four pretrained speech encoders (AfriHuBERT 94M, MMS-300M, MMS-1B and w2v-BERT-2.0 600M) are fine-tuned with CTC. A language-ID token is prepended to each target sequence and trained with the same loss, so no separate classification head is needed.

## 6. Experimental setup

Baselines: Whisper (small, medium, large-v3), SeamlessM4T-v2, MMS-1B-all and OmniASR (CTC and LLM variants, 300M to 7B). Out-of-domain evaluation on FLEURS, plus analyses of gender bias, vowel length and gemination, and training dynamics.

## 7. Dataset

The Ethiopian subset of the WAXAL corpus: about 1,106 hours in total, with roughly 181–197 training hours per language.

## 8. Evaluation metrics

Word error rate (WER) for transcription and accuracy for language identification.

## 9. Results

- The best model (w2v-BERT-2.0) reaches an **average WER of 30.48%**, ahead of the best OmniASR model (32.21%), which has about ten times the parameters.
- Per-language WER: roughly 23% Amharic, 24% Oromo, 32% Sidaama, 35% Tigrinya and 38% Wolaytta.
- Language identification accuracy is above 99.8% for all models.
- Normalising vowel length and gemination cuts WER by about 10–13 points, so these contrasts cause a large share of errors.
- Models learn language identification first, then Latin-script transcription, then Ge'ez-script transcription.

## 10. Limitations

OmniASR may have seen data overlapping the WAXAL test split, FLEURS references are noisy for some languages (native speakers judged 36.1% of Oromo FLEURS recordings unintelligible), gender imbalance in training data drives performance gaps, and end-to-end models capture vowel length and gemination poorly.

## 11. Future research directions

Explicit modelling of vowel length and gemination, and more demographically balanced data collection.
